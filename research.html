NanoBioMat / Research

Research

The NanoBioMat team studies iron oxide nanoparticles and their assemblies.

Our research covers nanomagnetism and biomedical applications. We connect nanoparticle synthesis, structure and properties. We also study their interactions with living systems.

01

Particles

Size, shape and anisotropy

Size, shape and structure influence the magnetic response of nanoparticles.

We synthesize nanoparticles with controlled sizes and shapes. We seek to tune their magnetic anisotropy. Their magnetic energy depends on the direction of their magnetization.

We study the links between synthesis, structure and magnetization dynamics. We modify particle surfaces to control dispersion and prepare assemblies.

Our thermal decomposition syntheses build on Sun & Zeng, 2002 (new tab) and Hou et al., 2007 (new tab).

02

Assemblies

Organization and magnetic interactions

Nanoparticles interact within assemblies. Their collective response can differ from that of dispersed particles.

We assemble nanoparticles and organic molecules. We vary their composition and formation conditions. We characterize their internal organization.

We study the effects of particle spacing, orientation and assembly compactness. We measure magnetic response and energy dissipation under alternating fields. We explore applications in imaging and magnetic activation.

We use flash nanoprecipitation (Johnson & Prud’homme, 2003 (new tab)). Our confined impinging jets (CIJ) mixer is adapted from Han et al., 2012 (new tab).

03

Biological interfaces

Interactions and biological fate

Proteins and cells interact with nanoassemblies. These interactions can change their surface, organization and properties.

We study cellular uptake of nanoassemblies with our partners. We track their transformation and degradation. We examine the effects of their composition and coating.

We explore their functions in imaging and active-molecule delivery. We study their activation by magnetic fields or light. Our projects address cancer and immune responses in liver diseases. We use cellular and preclinical models.
